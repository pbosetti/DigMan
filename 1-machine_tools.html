Manufacturing systems

To give shape to a block of raw material (workpiece) there are three possibilities:

  • Deform it: forming processes (motion axes: 1+)
  • Cut it: material removal (or subtractive) processes (motion axes: 2.5+)
  • Build on it: additive manufacturing processes (motion axes: 2.5+)

Manufacturing systems

  • We skip over forming processes, where motion planning and control is trivial (with exceptions!)
  • In subtractive and additive manufacturing there is a tool performing the process (a mill, a polarized wire, an extruding nozzle, …), whose motion is performed by synchronized action of two or more axes, typically in cartesian arrangement
  • The motion (trajectory and speed) must be planned according to geometry and process specifics

Subtractive Manufacturing: 5-axes milling

Additive Manufacturing: 5-axes Direct Laser Deposition

Reference case: Turning/Milling

  • The majority of products involve machining, either directly, or indirectly (dies are machined)
  • Machining involves a combination of linear and rotary motions:
    • In turning, the workpiece is rotating, the tool is displacing
    • In drilling and milling the tool is rotating and there is a combined relative displacement between tool and workpiece

Important

Regardless the process, understanding its physics and the governing parameters is essential to design the manufacturing system and its control algorithms

The subtractive manufactiring case

Material removal processes and their control parameters

Machining parameters

  • Mechanism of chip formation depends
    • on the relative speed of tool and workpiece (cutting speed)
    • on the cross section of removed material
  • Cutting speed and cross section (tool parameters) depend in turn on trajectory, rotary and translation speeds (machining parameters)

Turning

Machining parameters to be calculated are spindle speed, feed rate, and spindle power:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Rotational speed:}\quad & N = \frac{v}{\pi D_0} \\ \text{Diameter:}\quad & D_f = D_0 - 2d \\ \text{Feed rate:}\quad & f = N f_r \\ \text{Machining time:}\quad & T_m = \frac{L}{f} = \frac{\pi D_0 L}{f_r v} \\ \text{Material Removal Rate:}\quad & MRR = v f_r d \end{aligned} \]

Drilling

Machining parameters to be calculated are spindle speed, feed rate, and spindle power:

\[ \begin{aligned} N &= \frac{v}{\pi D} \\ f &= N f_r \\ T_m &= \frac{t + A}{f} \\ A &= \frac{D}{2}\tan\left(\frac{\pi - \theta}{2}\right) \\ MRR &= \frac{\pi D^2 f}{4} \end{aligned} \]

Milling

Machining parameters to be calculated are spindle speed, feed rate, and spindle power. There are three types of milling:

Milling

\[ \begin{aligned} N &= \frac{v}{\pi D} \\ f &= N f_z z \\ MRR &= w d f \\ A &= \begin{cases} \sqrt{d(D-d)} & \text{side milling} \\ \sqrt{w(D-w)} & \text{face/end milling} \end{cases} \\ T_m &= \begin{cases} (L + A)/f & \text{side milling} \\ (L + 2A)/f & \text{face/end milling} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]

Example

We have to set up a milling process on Aluminum, which has \(p_s = 12.56\) W min/cm³

The tool manufacturer suggests \(v = 1000\) m/min and \(f_z = 0.1\) mm/tooth

The mill has a diameter of \(30\) mm and does face milling with full overlapping, has \(3\) teeth and removes a layer of \(2\) mm

\[ \begin{aligned} N &= \frac{v}{\pi D} = \frac{1000\ \text{m/min}}{\pi\, 30\ \text{mm}} = 10610\ \text{rpm} \\ f &= f_z z N = 0.1\ \text{mm} \cdot 3 \cdot 10610\ \text{rpm} = 3183\ \text{mm/min} \\ MRR &= w d f = 30\ \text{mm} \cdot 2\ \text{mm} \cdot 3183\ \text{mm/min} = 190980\ \text{mm}^3/\text{min} \\ P &= MRR\, p_s = 190.98\ \text{cm}^3/\text{min} \cdot 12.56\ \text{W min/cm}^3 \simeq 2.4\ \text{kW} \end{aligned} \]

Note

The machininst must always ensure that the required maximum power does not exceet that available at the spindle. If not, some of the machining parameters must be reduced (usually \(d\) and or \(w\))

Cutting Economics

The cost of machining depends on:

  • labor: hourly cost of trained personnel
  • machinery: purchase cost / machine life
  • raw material: cost of initial workpiece
  • consumables: tools, cutting fluids, disposable fixtures

Some costs depend on process parameters (mainly cutting speed)

Tool wear

Tool wear

Tools are subject to wear. It can affect two parts of the tool:

  • the flank: it changes the geometry of the tool where it contacts the workpiece, so it progressively affects surface quality and dimensional accuracy
  • the rake face: it changes the geometry where the tool contacts the chip: it has no effect on the workpiece, but in the long run it weakens the tool and causes it to break

If the tool and the cutting parameters are chosen correctly, flank wear occurs before rake face wear

The wear law was formulated by Taylor: \(VT^n=K\), where:

  • \(V\) is the cutting speed
  • \(T\) is the tool life
  • \(n\) and \(K\) are material constants; \(K\) also depends on the end-of-life criterion (application specific)

Taylor’s law

\(VT^n=K \rightarrow \log(V) - n\log(T) = \log(K) \rightarrow \log(T) = \frac{1}{n}\log(V) - \frac{1}{n}\log(K)\)

Productivity optimization

The costs of machining production are related to:

  • investments and overhead (fixed)
  • labor and raw materials (variable)
  • cost per piece (variable)

Costs are dependent variables. The process parameters are independent parameters

Important

We can ask whether there are process conditions that maximize productivity and/or minimize costs

Productivity optimization — Turning

Symbol Meaning
\(C_p\) Cost per piece
\(C_m\) Machining cost
\(C_s\) Setup cost
\(C_l\) Loading cost
\(C_t\) Tooling cost
\(L\) Labor cost
\(O\) Overhead

In turning: \[ T_m = \frac{L}{fN}=\frac{\pi LD}{fV} \\ N_p = \frac{T}{T_m} = \frac{fK^{1/n}}{\pi LDV^{1/n-1}} \]

\[\begin{align} C_p &= C_m + C_s + C_l + C_t \\ C_m &= T_m(L_m + O_m) \\ C_l &= T_l(L_m + O_m) \\ C_t &= \frac{1}{N_p}\left[T_c(L_m+O_m) + T_g(L_g+O_g) + D_c\right] = \frac{C_{t0}}{N_p} \\ T_p &= T_{s,l} + T_m + \frac{C_{t0}}{N_p} \end{align}\]

Note

We have \(C_p=C_p(V)\) and \(T_p = T_p(V)\), so we can differentiate both to identify an optimal condition

Productivity optimization — Turning

Costs: \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial C_p}{\partial V} &= 0 \\ V_{o,c} &= \frac{K(L_m+O_m)^n}{[(1/n-1)C_{t0}]^n} \\ T_{o,c} &= (1/n -1)\frac{C_{t0}}{L_m+O_m} \end{align}\]

Times: \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial T_p}{\partial V} &= 0 \\ V_{o,t} &= \frac{K}{[(1/n-1)T_c]^n} \\ T_{0,t} &= (1/n -1)T_c \end{align}\]

Note

What do we optimize? Costs or times?

Productivity optimization — Turning

  • The optimal cutting speed \(V_{o,c}\) corresponds to maximum economic efficiency
  • However, when there are penalties for delays it may be worth producing at \(V_{o,t}\), because it is the speed that maximizes productivity
  • So, \(V\) shall be normally set to \(V_{o,c}\), increasing it up to (but not above) \(V_{o,t}\) if there are penalties for delays

Limits of analytical process models

Orthogonal cutting

  • Orthogonal cutting rarely applies. In most real machining operations chip formation is inherently 3-D
  • The chip formation model is idealized. In reality:
    • the cutting edge is never perfectly sharp
    • the shear plane does not have zero thickness
    • deformation does not occur only on the shear plane, but also on the machined surface in contact with the cutting edge and between chip and tool
  • The stress and strain field cannot be computed analytically
  • There is no reliable thermal model that accounts for friction and plastic deformation as heat sources
  • The strain rates involved \(\dot\gamma\) are extreme: this makes it very difficult to identify the plastic properties of the material under similar conditions

Limits of analytical process models

Taylor’s law

  • It is an empirical model, i.e., it has no physical basis but rests on observation alone, so it cannot be calibrated from measurements of material properties
  • It is an approximate law: more complete empirical laws exist in the form \(VT^nd^xf^y=K\), which account for other process parameters that have a much smaller influence than \(V\) but still affect tool life
  • It is expensive to calibrate:
    • the experimental procedure is necessarily slow and costly
    • \(n\) depends on the workpiece material and on the tool material
    • \(K\) depends on the end-of-life criterion, so it is user-specific (i.e., it cannot be found in tables)
  • Even the more complete laws do not account for factors such as lubrication and cooling

Economic optimization

  • The analysis above depends on being able to express \(T_m(V)\) analytically
    • This is possible only for elementary operations (e.g., cylindrical turning). For more complex operations the relation can only be computed point by point by simulating the process (via CAM or CNC functions, which entails considerable time and cost)
    • It relies on the availability of a validated and reliable wear law
    • It is worthwhile only for large and very large batches (economy of scale)

Actual control schemes

PLC: Programmable Logic Controller

PLC is a controller taht implemets logic relationships between inputs and outputs. It provides a range of interfaces and registers:

  • Analog and digital I/O (> 2048)
  • Relay (1000)
  • Memories (200)
  • Timers (200)
  • Counters (200)

Note

Can be either a separate hardware, or a software process running on the same computer that runs the CNC

PLC

  • Beside GPIO, PLC can also have advanced I/O and communication capabilities:
    • General purpose communication: RS232, RS422, Ethernet
    • Deterministic communication: Profibus, DeviceNet, Modbus, Industrial EtherNET, CAN-BUS
    • Special I/O: counters, virtual cams, PIDs and controllers
  • PLC programming languages have been designed to facilitate the transition form wired logics to software logics: the languages had to be intuitive and immediately productive for technicians with different backgrounds: electrical engineering, control engineering, firmware programming

PLC

The programming standard IEC 61131-3 provided this transition:

Language Type Description
Ladder Logic Graphical Resembles relay logic diagrams, intuitive for electricians
Function Block Diagram Graphical Resembles block diagrams, intuitive for control engineers
Sequential Function Chart Graphical Resembles flow charts and finite state machines, intuitive for process engineers
Structured Text Textual Resembles Pascal, intuitive for software engineers
Instruction List Textual Resembles assembly, intuitive for firmware engineers

Note

The standard states that languages must be interchangeable, i.e., the same program can be written in any language and must have the same behavior, different parts of a program can be written in different languages, and the program can be converted from one language to another

PLC: Ladder diagram

Note

  • On the left side there are contactors (inputs), on the right side there are coils (outputs).
  • parallel branches represent OR logic, series branches represent AND logic; contactors can be normally open or normally closed (NOT).
  • The program is read from left to right, top to bottom. The program is executed in a cyclic scan: all inputs are read, the logic is evaluated, and the outputs are updated.

PLC: Function Block Diagram

Note

  • Each block implements a function, which can be a logic function, a timer, a counter, or even a more complicated one as a PID controller
  • Appeals to control engineers, being quite similar to Simulink and other block-diagram-based control design software
  • Complex programs tend to become quite messy, so FBD is often used in combination with other languages and limited to implement specific functions related to control and signal processing

PLC: Sequential Function Chart

Note

  • SFC is a graphical language that implements a finite state machine
  • Each block is a state, each line a transition with its activation condition
  • It is very intuitive for process engineers, and it is often used in combination with other languages to implement the overall logic of a process, while the other languages are used to implement the details of each state

(Computer) Numerical Control

The CNC controller is the brain of the machine tool. It implements the judgement (A4) of Amber’s scale, and it is responsible for:

  • parsing the part-program
  • converting it into motion commands for the machine tool actuators
  • provinding action inputs to the PLC controller for auxiliary functions (coolant, tool change rutines, etc.)

CNC controllers are characterized by:

  • Types of motion interpolation
  • Number of controlled axes

Types of motion interpolation

CNC can control axes with increasing levels of sophistication:

  • Point to point: only the start and end points are controlled, the path is not controlled (drilling, pick-and-place)
  • Paraxial: one axis at a time can be controlled in feed (semi-automatic machines, machines that don’t need axes interpolation as sawing machines, presses)
  • Continuous: all axes can be controlled simultaneously, and the path is controlled in position and velocity

Number of controlled axes

  • 2 Axes: third axis is manually adjusted (manual lathes, manual mills)
  • 2 Axes and 1/2: only two axes at a time can be controlled, the third is for positioning
  • 3 Axes: full 3-D trajectory control of TCP
  • 5 Axes: full trajectory control of TCP and tool orientation

Coordinate systems

  • Axes references combine to give machine origin
  • Axes references are used to reset encoders (homing)
  • Machine controller works in machine coordinates
  • Workpiece origin is set up by the operator
  • Registration point is the reference to measure tool length and diameter
  • Support point is the reference to mount the tool on the spindle (when mounted, reference = support)

Coordinate transformations

  • The part program defines the toolpath as the trajectory of the tool center position (TCP) in the workpiece reference frame
  • The CNC controller converts the TCP coordinates into machine coordinates by applying a roto-translation transformation that accounts for:
    • the workpiece origin
    • the tool length
  • Tool diameter is accounted for by the tool radius compensation function, which offsets the toolpath by the tool radius in a direction perpendicular to the toolpath

Note

In turning, there is no tool radius compensation; rather, tool is measured by length and abuttment, the latter being the offset w.r.t. the registration point

Coordinate transformations

Any coordinate \(P_{wp}\) given in the workpiece reference frame \(O_{wp}\) is converted in machine coordinates \(P_m\), including tool length offset

\[ P_m = T_{wp \to m} P_{wp}; \qquad T_{wp \to m} = \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} & d_x \\ r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} & d_y \\ r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} & d_z + l_t \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

\(d_{x-z}\) are coordinates of \(O_{wp}\) in \(O_m\)

Coordinate transformations

Remember that the roto-translation matrix is:

\[ T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha\cos\beta & \cos\alpha\sin\beta\sin\gamma - \sin\alpha\cos\beta & \cos\alpha\sin\beta\cos\gamma + \sin\alpha\sin\gamma & d_x \\ \sin\alpha\cos\beta & \sin\alpha\sin\beta\sin\gamma + \cos\alpha\cos\beta & \sin\alpha\sin\beta\cos\gamma - \cos\alpha\sin\gamma & d_y \\ -\sin\beta & \cos\beta\sin\gamma & \cos\beta\cos\gamma & d_z \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The order of transformations is:

  1. Roll by \(\gamma\)
  2. Pitch by \(\beta\)
  3. Yaw by \(\alpha\)
  4. Translate by \((d_x, d_y, d_z)\)

Coordinate transformations (3-axes machines)

Machines with 3 controlled axes (no tool alignment) typically implement a simplified transformation that only accounts for the workpiece origin translation and rotation \(\theta\) about \(z\) and tool length:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_m \\ y_m \\ z_m \\1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 & d_x \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 & d_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & d_z + l_t \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Workplane tilt compensation

Even for 3 axes machines, it is customary to also include small rotations about \(x\) and \(y\) in the transformation matrix, to account for misalignments of the working plane and tool holder: this comes especially handy for 3-D printers

Measuring positions: touch probe

Reference positions (as \(O_{wp}\)can be measure in machine thanks to a touch probe:

  • a special non-rotating tool with a deflecting stylus and spherical tip that can be used to touch the workpiece and measure its position in machine coordinates
  • The 3 contact points provide a kinematic mount are also electric switches
  • When a switch opens, the current coordinate plus the sphere radius in the direction of motion gives the touch point coordinate

Tool reference (presetting)

Tool geometry can be measured in machine (exposed time) or by a tool presetter (masked time). The latter is preferred because it is faster and more accurate, and it allows to measure the tool geometry without having to mount it on the machine.

  • On-board presetting uses a fixed stylus/switched contact point in known position reachable by the tool
  • Tool presetting systems have a dummy spindle mount, a camera and a shadow projector

Part-program structure

The structure of a part program generally follows a common structure:

  • setups
  • roughing section: removes the bulk of the material, usually with a large tool and high feedrate, leaving a small allowance for finishing
  • finishing section: removes the remaining material with a small tool and low feedrate (final geometry)
  • reset of the machine to a safe position and end of program

Toolpaths

  • Roughing and finishing may require different tools for different geometries
  • Roughing typically follows simple, stepped trajectories
  • Roughing allowance (excess material) is usually in the range of
    • 0.5–1 mm for turning
    • 0.2–0.5 mm for milling

Workpiece Origin

In turning, X coordinates are in diameters. Two typical choices for origin:

  • rightmost axial point on finished workpiece
  • on the machine origin (fixtures must be taken into consideration)

Common choice for WP origin among machinists is the rightmost axial point on finished workpiece (turning) and topmost point on the finished workpiece (milling). This choice allows to quickly spot in a program safe fast motions (positive \(z\)), from dangerous fast motions (negative \(z\)) that can crash the tool into the workpiece or fixture.

CNC

Algorithms for Computer Numerical Control — machining complex shapes

CNC kernel tasks

The CNC kernel is the part of the CNC controller that implements the real-time control loop. It is responsible for:

  • Understanding a part-program (parsing)
  • Generate velocity profiles (profiling)
  • Position interpolation (project trajectoryb on the machine axes)
  • Generate command signals (actuation) to the axes drives

Note

Beside these tasks, the rest of the CNC also takes care of the GUI, programs selection, editing and storing, networking, tables management (presets and tools), interaction with the PLC, and other non-real-time tasks. The CNC kernel is the part of the CNC that is real-time and must be implemented in a real-time operating system (RTOS)

Profiling

Profiling is where motion algorithms are implemented. For every block:

  • preserve block length: \(\displaystyle\int_{t_a}^{t_b} f(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau = l\)
  • limit acceleration to the maximum allowed by the machine
  • limit jerks, to minimize vibrations and mechanical stress

Profiling

Discrete time-base (clock steps of \(t_q\)) and feedrate quantization. Block durations must be scaled to a multiple of \(t_q\) yet maintaining the same block length. The actual feedrate is reduced accordingly.

\[ \Delta t_1 = \frac{f_m - f_s}{a}, \quad \Delta t_2 = \frac{f_m - f_e}{d}, \quad \Delta t_m = \frac{l}{f_m} - \Delta t_1 \frac{f_m + f_s}{2 f_m} - \Delta t_2 \frac{f_e + f_m}{2 f_m} \]

\[ \Delta t^* = \mathrm{int}\left(\frac{\Delta t}{t_q} + 1\right) t_q = \Delta t_1 + \Delta t_m^* + \Delta t_2, \qquad \Delta t_m^* = \Delta t_m + \Delta t^* - \Delta t \]

Profiling

Discrete time-base (clock steps of \(t_q\)) and feedrate quantization. Block durations must be scaled to a multiple of \(t_q\) yet maintaining the same block length. The actual feedrate is reduced accordingly.

\[ \int_{t_s}^{t_e} f(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau = \frac{\Delta t_1}{2}(f_m^* + f_s) + \Delta t_m^* f_m^* + \frac{\Delta t_2}{2}(f_m^* + f_e) = l \]

\[ f_m^* = \frac{2l - f_s \Delta t_1 - f_e \Delta t_2}{\Delta t_1 + \Delta t_2 + 2\Delta t_m^*}, \qquad a^* = \frac{f_m^* - f_s}{\Delta t_1}, \qquad d^* = \frac{f_e - f_m^*}{\Delta t_2} \]

Profiling

In short segments there’s no time enough to reach the nominal feedrate: \(\Delta t_m \le 0\)

\[ \int_{t_s}^{t_e} f(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau = \frac{a\Delta t_1^2 + d\Delta t_2^2}{2}; \quad a\Delta t_1 = d\Delta t_2 \]

\[ \frac{\Delta t_1^2 (a + a^2/d)}{2} = l \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta t_1 &= \sqrt{\frac{2l}{a + a^2/d}} \\ \Delta t_2 &= \frac{a}{d}\Delta t_1 \\ \Delta t_2^* &= \Delta t^* - \Delta t_1 \end{aligned} \qquad f_m^*(\Delta t_1 + \Delta t_2^*) = 2l \qquad \begin{aligned} a^* &= \frac{f_m^* - f_s}{\Delta t_1} \\ d^* &= \frac{f_e - f_m^*}{\Delta t_2^*} \end{aligned} \]

Motion interpolation

  • The speed profile is evaluated and integrated at each time tick: \(\lambda(t) = 1/L\int_0^t f(\tau)\,d\tau\)
  • The axes set-points are calculated with the parametric equation of the path
  • For a linear motion:

\[ \begin{aligned} X(t) &= X_0 + \Delta X \lambda(t) \\ Y(t) &= Y_0 + \Delta Y \lambda(t) \\ Z(t) &= Z_0 + \Delta Z \lambda(t) \end{aligned} \]

Motion interpolation

For arc interpolation:

\[ \begin{aligned} X(t) &= X_c + R\cos(\vartheta_0 + \Delta\vartheta \lambda(t)) \\ Y(t) &= Y_c + R\sin(\vartheta_0 + \Delta\vartheta \lambda(t)) \\ Z(t) &= Z_0 + \Delta Z \lambda(t) \end{aligned} \]

Warning: feedrate may be reduced

\[ f_{m,a} = \min\left(f_m, \sqrt{aR}\right) \]

Motion interpolation

Two alternative G-code syntax for arcs:

  • arch center: G[02|03] X[\(X_f\)] Y[\(Y_f\)] I[\(I\)] J[\(J\)]
  • radius: G[02|03] X[\(X_f\)] Y[\(Y_f\)] R[\(\pm R\)] (radius sign discriminates which arc to take)

\[ \begin{aligned} \vartheta_0 &= \cos^{-1} I/R \\ \vartheta_f &= \cos^{-1} (X_f - X_c)/R \\ X_c &= X_0 + I \\ Y_c &= Y_0 + J \\ R &= \sqrt{I^2 + J^2} \\ R^2 &= (X_f - X_c)^2 + (Y_f - Y_c)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Motion interpolation

Center coordinates (two alternatives depending on the sign of \(R\))

\[ \Delta x = X_f - X_0, \qquad \Delta y = Y_f - Y_0 \]

\[ X_c = X_0 + \frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta x - \mathrm{sign}(R)\,\frac{\sqrt{-\Delta y^2 (\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2)(-4R^2 + \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2)}}{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}\right) \]

\[ Y_c = Y_0 + \frac{\Delta y}{2} + \mathrm{sign}(R)\,\frac{\Delta x \sqrt{-\Delta y^2 (\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2)(-4R^2 + \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2)}}{2\Delta x^2 \Delta y + 2\Delta y^3} \]

Jerk

Jerk-limited profiling:

  • corners in feedrate imply discontinuous acceleration
  • discontinuous acceleration mean infinite jerk
  • infinite jerk produces vibrations and mechanical stress
  • for limited jerk profiling each segment is split into 7 ranges (vs. 3)

Look-ahead feedrate profile

The above presented algorithm assumes that the instant speed goes to zero at each corner. This is not acceptable in real CNC machines, for two reasons:

  • Problem 1: tangent or semi-tangent blocks would result in excessive reduction of feedrate wrt nominal
  • Problem 2: it shall be possible to accelerate to nominal feedrate with continuous acceleration over \(n\) short segments, and it may take more than one segment to decelerate down to stop

To solve these issue we need to plan the velocity profile across multiple blocks of G-Code. This is called look-ahead

Look-ahead feedrate profile

Approach:

  1. Calculate \(v_i\), \(v_m\), \(v_f\) for each block, considering \(v = v(s)\)
  2. Forward step: from block 1 to block \(n\), calculate accelerations only
  3. Backward step: from block \(n\) to block 1, calculate decelerations only and compose with accelerations
  4. Change variable \(s \to t\) and get \(v = v(t)\)
  5. Reshape \(v^* = \dfrac{v(t)}{k}\), where \(k = t_f / t_f^*\) and \(t_f^*\) is the multiple of \(t_q\) immediately larger than the total time \(t_f\) (block by block)

Constant Acc/Dec

  • We cannot calculate feed profiles in time abscissa, for we don’t know the duration of each step
  • Thus we start planning in curvilinear abscissa \(s\):
    • \(s(t) = s_0 + v_0 t + a t^2/2\), and thus \(t(s) = \dfrac{1}{a}\left(-v_0 \pm \sqrt{2a(s - s_0) + v_0^2}\right)\)
    • Since \(v(t(s)) = v_0 + a\, t(s)\), it follows that \(v(s) = \sqrt{2a(s - s_0) + v_0^2}\)
    • So accelerations and decelerations follow a square root law of \(s\)
  • In jerk-limited approach, we’d have a cubic root branch at the beginning and at the end of each acc/dec branch

Look-ahead feedrate

Step 1: calculate velocities:

  • \(v_{i,i} = v_{f,i-1}\), or 0 for first sg.
  • \(v_{m,i} = f_i\) for G01
  • \(v_m = \min(f_i, \sqrt{aR})\) for G02/G03
  • \(v_{f,i} = \dfrac{v_{m,i} + v_{m,i+1}}{2}\cos\alpha_i\)
  • \(v_{f,i} = 0\) for last segment or if \(v_{m,i} = 0\)

Forward step: accelerations

Long segments, \(v_i < v_m\):

A. \(v_f > v_m\), \(s_0 < s_1 < s_2 < s_f\)

B. \(v_f < v_m\), \(s_0 < s_1 < s_f < s_2\)

Forward step: accelerations

Long segments, \(v_i > v_m\):

A. \(v_f > v_m\), \(s_1 < s_0 < s_2 < s_f\)

B. \(v_f < v_m\), \(s_1 < s_0 < s_f < s_2\)

Forward step: accelerations

Short segments:

  • \(s_0 < s_f < s_1\)
  • \(v_f\) and \(s_2\) are not relevant

Forward step: accelerations

Short segments:

  • If \(v_i\) is large enough, fallback to long segment cases

Backward step: decelerations

  • Same approach as forward step
  • For each block, select the path going from \((s_0, v_i)\) to \((s_f, v_f)\)

Change of variable

Back to time abscissa

Convert back from curvilinear abscissa to time, one block at a time:

  • Calculate \(v(t) = v_0 + at\), \(s(t) = s_0 + v_0 t + at^2/2\) in the ranges \([s_i, s_1]\), \([s_1, s_2]\), \([s_2, s_f]\)
  • Calculate timings \(t_1\), \(t_2\), \(t_f\)

Reshape for discrete time

  • Calculate \(t_f^* = \lceil t_f / t_q + 1 \rceil t_q\)
  • Calculate \(k = t_f^* / t_f\)
  • Rescale feedrate so that \(v^*(t) = \dfrac{v(t)}{k}\)

Receding horizon

  • This algorithm requires a sequence of blocks starting and ending at zero feedrate
  • You can assume that G00 blocks mark full stops at corners
  • Nonetheless, a program can consist in thousands of sequential G01/G02/G03 blocks: memory problem
  • Solution:
    • At run time, pre-elaborate a buffer made of a limited number of blocks (say 100) assuming the last one stops (look-ahead buffer)
    • Re-calculate the moving buffer as soon as a block is done by adding a new block at the end (FIFO): start backward (dec) until no change, then forward (acc)
    • The buffer size depends on available memory and on computation time (must be shorter than the time needed to execute the buffer); calculations are done in parallel in a separate thread

Errors in machine tools

Machine tools are precision systems — laser interferometry

Machining error sources

  • Process error sources: tool/workpiece interaction (minimized by proper process conditions)
  • Structural error sources: time-dependent, eventually become stable and can be compensated
  • Machine error sources: more difficult to compensate, potentially ever changing

Error Compensation (A5)

Compensating for error means to measure errors and to apply corrections to the machine tool commands. This is a closed-loop control approach

  • Adaptive control systems (A/C) = evaluation attribute (A5)
  • Factors under evaluation:
    • forces/deflections
    • machining errors
    • thermoelastic dilatations/deformations

Profiling errors

Look-ahead can alter trajectory, especially at corners:

  • More evident at high speed
  • When a sharp corner is needed, use curls or disable look-ahead

Feedrate-related errors

Positioning errors

Squareness errors

Elementary errors

  • Three linear errors per axis (linearity, straightness, undulation)
  • Three angular errors per axis (roll, pitch, yaw)
  • Two squareness errors between axes 1 and 2
  • Two squareness errors between axes 3 and 2
  • Total of 18 + 4 = 22 elementary errors, 18 of which are position-dependent

Laser interferometer

Laser interferometer

Linearity measurement

  • This is the simnplest measurement
  • The laser beam is reflected by a mirror mounted on the axis and the interferometer measures the distance travelled by the axis
  • It is a way to calibrate and to verify the positioning accuracy of the axis encoder

Angle measurement

  • An angle interferometer only changes the optical paths difference upon rotation
  • Rotation can happen either on the splitter or in the retro-reflector
  • The action plane can be selected to measure the pitch or the yaw angles

Straightness measurement

A Wollaston prism is a beam-splitting prism that splits a beam of light into two beams with orthogonal polarizations. The two beams are diverging at a small angle (typically 15–30 arcmin). The prism is used to measure the straightness of an axis by measuring the lateral displacement of the reflected beam.

Straightness measurement

  • By aligning the wollastone prism action plane with XY and with XZ planes it is possible to measure the straightness of the X axis in Y and Z directions

Squareness measurement

  • By combining lateral displacement measurement with an optical square it is possible to measure the squareness between two axes.
  • The optical square is a reflecting prism that reflects the laser beam at 90° its incoming direction

Calibration

Laser interferometer: three-axis machine tool installation

High precision machine tools are equipped with a laser interferometer to measure the actual position of the axes.

  • one laser head (emitter only)
  • one receiver per axis
  • beam splitters direct 1/3 of the toutal source power to each axis

Note

Laser beam can be affected by refraction index changes and by air turbulence, so the machine is usually equipped with a temperature and pressure sensor to correct the measured distance and the laser is usually enclosed in a protective tube to avoid air turbulence.
